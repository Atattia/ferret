Tuesday, after closing

Mara counted eleven empty desks on the third floor. In February there had
been two. Nobody had moved downstairs, and the coat hooks beside those
desks were bare even on rainy mornings.

At first, each departure came with a cake and a card. By June, people just
left their badges beside the kettle. The remaining names kept appearing
in more columns on the weekly rota. Tasks that once belonged to three
people now landed with one, while the promised replacements never arrived.

The director suggested another pizza evening. Mara remembered the last
one: six unopened boxes, a room full of glowing screens, and nobody with
time to stop. Leon had missed his daughter's bedtime four nights that
week. Priya had stopped offering ideas in meetings; she said it was easier
to nod and get through the day.

On Friday, Mara found two more envelopes under the director's door. Both
writers thanked their colleagues. Neither asked for more money. One wanted
her Sundays back. The other wanted to finish a day without feeling that
he had let everybody down.

Mara put away the catering leaflet. Before ordering anything else, she
drew three columns on a sheet of paper: what could wait, what could stop,
and who would take over when someone went home.
